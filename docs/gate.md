# The gate

A sandbox reaches public IPv4 addresses over TCP, and one DNS resolver, by the Interface in use, while a probe answers with an exit address expected of that Interface and the Host Conditions hold. By a way out where you say that names are answered with stand-in addresses, it reaches those too. It reaches nothing else, and nothing while its gate is locked.

The sandbox's own `127.0.0.1` works whatever the gate says, so dev servers and headless browsers inside it are unaffected by the gate.

## How the gate holds

A sandbox is two containers that share a network namespace, and one program on this Mac: the **gatekeeper**, which has `NET_ADMIN` and nothing else; the **workstation**, which has no capabilities at all and runs as UID 1000; and the sandbox's **Carrier**.

- **The Allowed Interfaces.** You list the interfaces of this Mac a sandbox may leave by, most preferred first: `en0`, a tunnel that another app of yours keeps up, whatever has a name. The **Interface in use** is the first of them that is up with an address. For each you say what exit addresses you expect there, or that any will do, or any but physical, or physical and no other: the address this Mac's Wi-Fi or Ethernet exits at with nothing between, which the far end of a tunnel is not. The form calls them what the sandbox **leaves by**, and nobody types a name there: you choose among the interfaces that are up and lead somewhere now, each under what the system calls it (`Wi-Fi · en0`), or by its name where it has no title, as a tunnel.
- **The namespace is on no network of Docker's.** It holds loopback and one **TUN device**. `tun`, a second process in the gatekeeper's container, makes the device and moves its packets, each behind its length, to and from the app over the one connection Docker gives a command it runs: the **Packet stream**. The workstation can neither open the device nor change it, nor make another.
- **The Carrier.** For each running sandbox the app runs one Carrier, which is given the Packet stream. It ends the sandbox's TCP connections on this Mac and makes each of them again on a socket bound to the Interface in use; a name query it sends on from such a socket too. It dials public addresses only, but for the stand-in addresses below, and listens on nothing. It runs under the **Carrier Wall**, a Seatbelt profile that leaves it connections out and nothing else of this Mac: no file of yours, no other program, no listening socket, no address of this Mac itself.
- **A change of Interface is a lock first.** When the Interface in use changes, the Carrier carries nothing by the new one until the gatekeeper has been told, has ended the lease and has said so: no packet leaves by one Interface on a lease that was earned on another. While none of the Allowed Interfaces is up, the gate is locked for **No Interface**, the gatekeeper does not probe, and nothing of the sandbox leaves this Mac at all. The probes that are on their way when the gatekeeper is told of another Interface in use, or of none, it ends at once: a connection that was still being made would go on asking.

- The gatekeeper loads one nftables ruleset before the device exists, and never edits it. Everything not allowed is rejected.
- **The probe.** In a **round**, the gatekeeper asks each probe URL, over a new HTTPS connection, what address it was called from. The first round of an Interface in use it sends together; each one after that it spreads over two seconds, one probe URL after another at even distances, so that with three probe URLs a probe leaves every two thirds of a second. While a probe that is a second old has no answer, and none sent since has one, it asks each probe URL every second, whether or not the probes before were answered: a way out that answers at once is asked half as often as one that is slow or silent. A new sandbox starts with three probe URLs, of three operators. The probe's connections cross the device and the Carrier as the sandbox's do, so an answer is the exit address by way of the Interface in use. The first answer with an address you expect of that Interface, or with any address where you said any will do, or with any IPv4 address that is not one of this Mac's physical exit addresses where you said that, opens the gate or keeps it open. An answer with another address locks the gate at once, and for five seconds from when that probe was sent no answer opens it. A probe nobody answers changes nothing. Where a probe URL's name is, the gatekeeper looks up once and then again every minute, beside the probes: a probe does not wait for a name query.
- "Open" is a **lease**: one element of an nftables set, with a timeout, that each such answer renews to five seconds from when its probe was sent. Every packet the sandbox sends is a lookup of that element. When it is gone, each flow is reset at its next packet, and if the gatekeeper hangs or dies, or no probe is answered, the kernel lets it run out.
- **A Hold**, where a sandbox's Setup asks for one, is a second such element, with a rule that only such a sandbox's ruleset has. A new sandbox's Setup asks for one; a sandbox that was saved without keeps none until you switch it on. It lasts thirty seconds from when the last probe that was answered with an expected address was sent, and while it lasts the connections the sandbox has are kept though the lease ran out, on a way out whose probes are slow at times. The lease of such a gate is the shorter: an answer renews it, from when it came, for the time in which two more answers are due, each a round's two seconds divided by the number of probe URLs after the last, and three quarters of a second more: about two seconds with three probe URLs, so that one probe may be lost. As at any gate, it runs to no later than five seconds after its probe was sent. So by a way out that answers in less than half a second the gate is locked two and a half seconds after the last probe that was answered was sent, not five, and by one whose answers take three seconds it still opens. A lease that runs out there cuts nothing. Only the lease is shorter: an address that is not expected is believed for five seconds at any gate, and a heartbeat may be as late. What they receive still arrives: a download, an agent's answer. What the sandbox sends on them does not leave this Mac: the rule lets it through to the Carrier, where it waits, in the order it came, and leaves when the gate is open again. Nothing new is made under a Hold: a connection begins, and a name is asked, under the lease alone. A gate that keeps a Hold waits ten seconds for a probe, and an answer that came too late for the lease renews the Hold. Only silence leaves it: an answer with an address that is not expected ends it at once, and so does whatever else locks the gate for a reason it knows: a Host Condition, physical exit addresses that are not known, another Interface in use, heartbeats that stopped. Then every connection is reset, and what waited is dropped.
- **The Carrier sends on what the sandbox sends only under a lease it knows of.** Each time the lease is renewed the gatekeeper says how long it has to run, and the Carrier counts that from when it is told: past it, with no word since, what the sandbox sends waits, as under a Hold, whatever the ruleset let through. With no word for five seconds it lets go of every connection.
- **The gatekeeper carries nothing and listens on nothing**, and `tun` moves packets between the device and its standard streams and opens no connection. The namespace holds no NAT rule and no socket that the workstation could talk to. If `tun` or the Carrier ends, the sandbox ends with it.
- The probe's sockets are told apart by a socket mark, which takes `NET_ADMIN` to set. They reach port 443 of public addresses and the resolver, whatever the lease says, and nothing else; a root process in the workstation gets no such mark, and nothing in the sandbox can answer a probe in the endpoint's place. No mark crosses to the Carrier, which knows a probe's connection by where it comes from: the gatekeeper sends its probes from the **Probe Source Ports**, 61000 to 61999, above the ports the namespace hands out by itself, and the ruleset rejects what comes from one of them without the mark. A probe's connection waits for no lease: it is how the gate opens.
- While the gate is open, the sandbox sends **TCP to public IPv4 addresses** and **name queries to one resolver**: a public one named in its configuration (`1.1.1.1` unless you change it), not this Mac's own DNS, so that the sandbox does not learn names on your network. Other UDP (QUIC included), ICMP and IPv6 do not leave: the ruleset rejects them, and the Carrier would carry none of them. Addresses that are not public are refused twice over, by the ruleset and by the Carrier: private and link-local ranges, carrier-grade NAT, the benchmarking range `198.18.0.0/15`, multicast.
- **Stand-in addresses**, where you say so. Some tunnels answer a name with a stand-in address, one of the range `198.18.0.0/15`, in place of the name's own, and take a connection to it for one to the name. Where the Setup of a way out says that names are answered so there, the sandbox connects to that range too, while that is the Interface in use and by no other. Both judges are told: the ruleset has a set for the range, which holds it only then, and gains or loses it in the one transaction that ends the lease when the Interface in use changes; the Carrier carries to the range only by such an Interface, and never to an address this Mac itself has in it. The gatekeeper's probes connect to the stand-in address their probe URL's name is answered with there. A sandbox none of whose ways out says so has neither the set nor its rules.
- **Any exit address but a physical one** is for a tunnel whose exits change: you list nothing, and the one address that is refused is where the tunnel leads when it stops leading elsewhere, as when the app that keeps it falls back to going direct. A **physical exit address** is a public address the internet sees this Mac at by way of one of its physical Interfaces: one that is up with an IPv4 address, has a hardware address and has a default route through a router, other than the Interface in use. The **Host Watchdog**, in the app, reads them: it asks the sandbox's own probe URLs, as the gatekeeper asks them and with the same resolver and pin, on sockets bound to each such interface, every ten seconds, at once when an interface is new or has changed, and every two seconds while an interface has no answer that is believed. Every address answered in the last minute counts. The app tells the gatekeeper in each heartbeat which they are and whether it knows them, which is when every one of this Mac's physical Interfaces has answered within that minute. While it does not, the gate is locked for **Physical Exit Address Unknown**; an answer to the gatekeeper's probe with one of them, or over IPv6, is an address that is not expected. An address the gate is open on that turns out to be this Mac's physical exit address locks it by the heartbeat that says so. Nothing is asked from this Mac's physical Interfaces for a sandbox whose Interface in use does not expect this.
- The app sends the gatekeeper a **heartbeat** with the state of the Host Conditions, the Interface in use and, where that Interface expects this Mac's physical exit address or any but it, those. If the app quits, crashes or hangs, the gate locks, and stays locked; the Carrier, told nothing of the gate for as long as a lease lasts, lets go of every connection it holds for the sandbox. After any lock it resets them at once, so that what it had taken from the sandbox and not yet sent does not go on leaving.
- **A workstation's image is built in its sandbox's namespace.** The first start of a set of packs builds an image, and the steps of that build, which install packages and run a pack's commands, run where the workstation will: with loopback and the TUN device and no other, behind the sandbox's ruleset, by its Carrier and the Interface in use, while its gate is open. The gate is waited for, half a minute at most: a sandbox whose gate does not open builds no image and does not start, and says why its gate is locked.
- A second ruleset in the VM (the **backstop**) keeps every container bridge away from this Mac, the VM and every private network. Nothing of zeolite's is on a bridge: no sandbox, and no step of an image's build. The backstop stands behind whatever else the VM's Docker is asked to run. The app rebuilds it at launch, checks that it is intact before each sandbox starts, and leaves it in the VM when it quits.
- A sandbox is told nothing of where it runs: its hosts file, its resolver file, its hostname and its environment name no address but its own, loopback and its resolver, and neither Docker's nor the VM's name for this Mac.

## What it does not promise

- **An Interface is its name.** zeolite binds sockets to the name you gave, and whatever holds that name carries the sandbox. `en0` leaves from this Mac's own address whatever other app of yours runs. Where another app recreates its tunnel under another name, the sandbox has lost that Interface and goes on to the next you allowed, or locks.
- **An Interface that stays up and begins to lead elsewhere** is seen when a probe sent after that is answered: two seconds divided by the number of probe URLs later at most, two thirds of a second with three, and the time an answer takes. If none is answered, the lease runs out. Either way, for up to about five seconds the sandbox's new connections leave where it leads now. At a gate that keeps a Hold that is, where no probe is answered any more, as long as an answer takes and about two seconds more with three probe URLs: two and a half seconds by a way out that answers fast, and never more than five; what the sandbox sends on the connections it has leaves as long, and no longer. Where you said any exit address will do, it is never seen. Where you said any but physical, it is seen when it leads to this Mac's physical Interface, in the same time, and never when it leads anywhere else. Where you said physical, it is seen when it leads anywhere but there. This Mac's physical exit addresses are those its physical Interfaces answered with in the last minute, read every ten seconds: a Mac whose network exits at an address that changes from one connection to the next will find that gate locked now and then, until the new address has been read.
- **Under a Hold, this Mac still answers for the connections it keeps**, for up to thirty seconds from the last probe that was answered as expected. Nothing the sandbox sends leaves then. But this Mac's own TCP acknowledges what such a connection receives, sends again what the Carrier had handed it before the lease ran out and was not yet acknowledged, and ends a connection the Carrier lets go of: those packets leave where the Interface leads now, and if that changed meanwhile they show the new address to the far end and to whoever is on the way. A connection cannot move to another exit address: its far end knows it by the address it was made at. A sandbox keeps a Hold only where its Setup asks for one, as a new sandbox's does: switch it off for a gate that keeps nothing once its lease is over.
- **A kept connection receives only as far as its protocol goes unanswered.** What the sandbox would send waits, an acknowledgement of its application's included: a download over HTTP/2 stops when its window is used up, and a far end that waits for a reply gives up when it likes. When the gate opens again, what waited leaves at once.
- **The Carrier is told of a lease a moment after the kernel has it, and of its end a moment after.** What the sandbox sends in between is sent on, or waits, by the Carrier's count: the time one line takes from the gatekeeper to the Carrier, by way of the app.
- **A sandbox sends nothing from the Probe Source Ports**, 61000 to 61999: a program in it that binds one of them for a connection out is refused. It may listen on them, on its loopback.
- **Software that takes over all of this Mac's traffic** (a network extension, a packet filter rule) can still send a bound socket another way. Only expected exit addresses catch that. It does the same to the sockets this Mac's physical exit addresses are read on: they are then read wrong, or not at all and the gate stays locked, and "any but physical" cannot be used beside it.
- **Any but physical is detection, not prevention**, and has limits of its own:
  - A tunnel that sends only some destinations direct is not seen, as with any expectation: the probe measures its own connections.
  - The operators of the probe URLs see both addresses. The gatekeeper asks them from the tunnel's exit and the app asks the same URLs from this Mac's own address, alike in shape and pace: an operator can tell that the two belong together.
  - This Mac's physical exit addresses are read over IPv4 only. An answer to the gatekeeper's probe over IPv6 locks the gate, since it cannot be held against them: if your tunnel's exit reaches a probe URL over IPv6, use probe URLs that answer over IPv4 only.
  - A change of this Mac's network that none of its interfaces shows is read within ten seconds; until then the new physical exit address is not refused.
  - Where this Mac's network leaves from more addresses than a minute of Rounds sees, some are not refused.
  - Every physical Interface must answer. One with a router that leads nowhere, such as a cable to a device without the internet, keeps the gate locked for Physical Exit Address Unknown, as does a Mac whose way out has no hardware address (PPPoE dialled on the Mac itself), which has no physical Interface at all.
  - With an automatic timezone, a sandbox for which this Mac's physical exit addresses stay unknown fails to start when the timezone's wait is over, as one whose probes go unanswered does.
- **The internet sees this Mac's TCP, not the sandbox's.** The Carrier makes each connection anew: what a server reads of the stack that called it is macOS, and what the sandbox set on its own sockets does not cross.
- **A Mac that sleeps ends the sandboxes' connections.** Before this Mac sleeps every gate is locked, and a lock ends every connection a sandbox has: a download that was running is cut, and is not taken up again when the Mac wakes. While a sandbox is starting or runs, the app keeps this Mac from sleeping by itself when it is idle, on battery too, where it would otherwise sleep a minute or so after your last key; its display still turns off. Closing the lid, or choosing Sleep, puts it to sleep all the same.
- **A way out by which no probe is answered costs connections.** The gate stays open while some probe is answered within five seconds of being sent. When none is, the lease runs out, and a lock ends every connection the sandbox has; the gate opens again at the next answer that matches.
- **With a Hold it costs a wait, and more often.** The gate stays open while an answer comes within about two seconds of the last, with three probe URLs: two probes lost in a row, or an answer that takes a second and a half longer than the one before, lock it until the next answer that matches. The connections the sandbox has are kept, and what it sends waits; a connection it tries to make meanwhile is refused at once, and a name is not resolved.
- **A probe that fails says nothing.** An endpoint that refuses, answers with an error or is cut off is a probe nobody answered: the gate does not lock for it, and stays open until its lease runs out.
- **The first answer opens the gate.** Where one endpoint sees an expected address and another does not, the gate is open from the first's answer to the second's. That is a fraction of a second when the sandbox starts and after a change of Interface, where a round is sent together; and whenever the second has not answered for five seconds, it is until its next probe is answered, two seconds at most and the time an answer takes.
- **The probes are requests to services that others run**, for as long as the sandbox runs: to each probe URL one every two seconds, 43,200 a day, by a way out where a probe is answered within a second, and one a second, 86,400 a day, where it is not. An endpoint that limits them is a probe nobody answered.
- **Connections are limited**: a sandbox holds 1024 connections and name queries at a time through its Carrier, and one more gets no answer.
- **The Carrier Wall is Seatbelt**, which macOS ships and Apple calls deprecated. The Carrier reads packets an agent wrote; the wall is what stands behind its own code. Without `sandbox-exec` no sandbox with a network starts.
- **The probe measures its own connections.** It reads the address that TCP connections it makes itself leave from. If what the Interface in use leads to sends some connections out from one address and others from another (by destination, by port, by time), what the probe saw is not what every connection of the sandbox gets.
- **The probe's request leaves whatever the gate says**, also while it is locked, by the Interface in use. That is inherent in checking the exit address. While the gate is locked, the probe's connections and its name queries are the only packets of the sandbox that leave this Mac.
- **Name queries are not measured.** They leave by the Interface in use, as UDP, and the probe is a TCP connection: where that Interface sends UDP from, the probe does not say.
- **A stand-in address leads where the tunnel says.** A sandbox cannot connect to an address that is not public: where what the Interface in use leads to answers names with stand-in addresses, names resolve and nothing connects, until you say in that way out's Setup that it answers so, or have it hand out the names' own addresses. Once you say so, where a stand-in address leads is decided where the way out leads, by the name it stands for, and zeolite does not see it. A name that is led to this Mac's loopback or to your own network there reaches it: by that way out, that a sandbox reaches public addresses alone is what the tunnel makes of it. See that what keeps the tunnel up sends everything the sandbox sends one way.
- **Only `198.18.0.0/15` is taken for stand-in addresses.** No host of the internet has an address there, and hardly a network of anyone's: opening it by a way out that hands out none reaches nothing. A tunnel that hands out stand-in addresses of another range that is not public is refused as before: have it use this one. One that hands out addresses that look public was never refused.
- **A stand-in address means something by one way out, and for a while.** It stands for a name only where it was handed out, for as long as that is remembered there. After the Interface in use changed it is refused, and once it was forgotten it leads to another name or to none: a program that kept one asks the name again. The gatekeeper asks where its probe URLs' names are every minute: where what it kept was forgotten meanwhile, its probes go unanswered until then, and the gate is locked that long.
- **`/workspace` is the sandbox's Home**, a folder of its own on the VM's disk: kept when the sandbox stops, deleted when it is destroyed. It has no size limit of its own: it fills the VM's disk, which every sandbox shares. What an agent logged in to rests there, readable by whoever reads the VM's disk. Stop still ends every process at once: a file that was being written may be half written at the next start. `/tmp` is on the VM's disk too, a volume made with each start and deleted with the stop, with no size limit of its own either. `/dev/shm` is memory, counts against the sandbox's memory limit, and goes with a stop.
- **An automatic timezone is that of the first exit address.** It is read once, before the workstation starts, and does not follow a change of Interface.
- **UDP, ICMP and IPv6 are dropped**: what an agent does over them fails.
- **Two things are fetched by none of the Allowed Interfaces.** The images a build starts from (`node:22-bookworm-slim`, `debian:bookworm-slim`), which Docker pulls by itself; and the two Debian packages of the gatekeeper's image, whose one build step runs on the VM's own network, since that image is what makes a sandbox's namespace. Both leave from the VM, wherever this Mac's network leaves from at that moment, behind no gatekeeper, no probe and no Carrier, and the backstop judges neither. The gatekeeper's step is zeolite's own command and Debian's signed packages; while it runs it reaches what the VM reaches, this Mac at the address the VM has for it among that. Each happens once in a VM, until what it made is deleted or the image changes.
- **A build's step is not a workstation.** It runs as root with the capabilities Docker gives the step of any build, raw sockets among them, where a workstation has none. With a raw socket a step can put packets on the device that the ruleset never judged. They reach the Carrier and nothing else, and it carries what it carries for a sandbox: TCP to public addresses and name queries to the resolver, by the Interface in use. But what comes from a Probe Source Port the Carrier takes for a probe's, and sends on whatever the gate says: a step that forges one is carried while the gate is locked. A pack's commands are code you chose to run as root in your sandbox's image; this is what it could do to the gate while that image is built.
- **A gate that locks while an image is built costs the build.** The step's connections are cut, or refused, as the sandbox's would be, and a package manager gives up: the start fails, and the next goes on from the steps that were done.
- **Root in the VM is outside the promise.** Something that got out of a container and became root in the VM reaches this Mac's loopback at the address the VM has for the Mac, and reads and writes every Shared Folder. Nothing on the Mac's side stops it.
- **A sandbox knows what kind of machine it is on.** It can tell that it runs in a Linux VM on Apple hardware, and it can read its resolver's address. What a Shared Folder adds to that is listed under [Shared Folders](shared-folders.md).
- **A key you give an agent is its sandbox's.** The app takes no API key, keeps none and hands none on: you log an agent in, or export its key, in its terminal. Everything in the sandbox can then read it, and it rests in the sandbox's Home.
